A History of Luxury Purses: Renaissance to Amber Clutches

Deconstructing 16th-century girdled sweetmeat pouches, Victorian silk reticules, Art Deco jewelry box minaudières, and contemporary Baltic amber clutches.

The handbag is far more than a functional receptacle for personal belongings; across five centuries of European and American fashion history, it has served as the ultimate wearable canvas of social identity, artistic expression, and artisanal virtuosity. From the embroidered girdle pouches of Renaissance Venice to the gemstone-encrusted vanity cases of Paris in the 1930s, the evolution of the luxury purse mirrors the shifting tides of art, architecture, and craftsmanship.

At Amber Purse Fox, our bespoke creations represent the latest chapter in this illustrious cultural chronicle. By uniting 40-million-year-old Baltic amber with traditional French leathercraft, we pay homage to the great historical eras of luxury accessory design. In this comprehensive chronicle, our fashion historians trace the fascinating lineage of the evening purse from the Renaissance to the modern amber clutch.

1. The Renaissance Girdle Pouch and Sweetmeat Purse (1450–1650)

How early purses emerged as public declarations of status:

  • The Almoner Pouch (*Aumônière*): Suspended from silk cords at the waist girdle, Renaissance purses were crafted from velvet, fine calfskin, and cloth-of-gold, embroidered with seed pearls and coat-of-arms heraldry.
  • Sweetmeat and Perfume Scent Pouches: Nobility carried miniature silk purses holding fragrant ambergris, dried lavender, and candied spices to ward off pestilence in bustling medieval cities.
  • Cast Bronze and Gilded Silver Clasp Frames: The first mechanical metal opening clasps appeared, featuring intricate hand-chased filigree and miniature lock mechanisms.

“The evening clutch is the jewelry of leathergoods. It sits directly in the hand, catching the light and completing the silhouette of couture fashion.”

2. The Regency and Victorian Reticule (1790–1900)

The birth of the handheld purse during the neoclassical revolution:

  1. The Abolition of Hidden Pockets: When French Directoire fashion introduced flowing, sheer neoclassical muslin gowns, women could no longer hide bulky under-skirt pockets, creating the urgent need for a handheld bag (*le réticule*).
  2. Beaded Micro-Mosaic Reticules: Victorian women spent hundreds of hours hand-threading thousands of microscopic glass beads to depict romantic landscapes and classical mythology.
  3. Chatelaines and Dance Purses: Gilded metal waist plaques suspended tiny leather dance card cases, ivory memorandum tablets, and tortoiseshell smelling-salt flasks at Victorian society balls.

3. The Golden Age of the Minaudière: Art Deco Paris (1920–1939)

How Charles Arpels revolutionized the luxury evening bag:

In 1930, high jewelry master Charles Arpels observed opera patron Florence Gould carrying her makeup in a crude metal cigarette tin. Inspired, he invented The Minaudière—a rigid, jewel-like metal evening case sculpted from precious gold, mother-of-pearl, and black lacquer with spring-loaded compartments for powder, lipstick, and opera glasses.

Frequently Asked Questions on Handbag History

What is the historical origin of the word "minaudière"?+
The term comes from the French verb *minauder* (to smile coyly or simper), named after the charming theatrical mannerisms of Madame Florence Gould, for whom the first jeweled case was created.
Were handbags originally carried by men or women?+
During the Middle Ages and Renaissance, girdle pouches were carried equally by men and women to hold coins and keys, before evolving into predominantly women's fashion accessories in the late 18th century.
